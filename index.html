Ten thousand years of inventing ships

Draw the whole story on one line. [click] Sails, about 5,000 years ago [verify]. [click] And everything else in this talk — steam, Brunel, Froude, computers, AI — is this sliver at the end.

Sails made ships bigger and voyages longer

The Batavia: a VOC ship of 1628 — this is the replica built in Lelystad. [click] Ships like her connected Texel to Batavia, today's Jakarta. [click] About eight months each way [verify]. And the trick was the wind: sail south, then let the Roaring Forties blow you east.

Steam made ships fast, reliable — and hungryCoal takes up space, and it isn't free

Steam: fast and reliable, no waiting for the wind. [click] But steam is HUNGRY — say it deliberately, we come back to it. Coal takes up space and isn't free: a ship to Australia had to stop for coal.

Brunel's bet: bigger ships are more efficient

Isambard Kingdom Brunel, in front of the launching chains of his biggest ship. [click] His bet: double the length of a box. [click] Its surface, which is what the water drags on, goes up 4×; its volume, for cargo and coal, goes up 8×. [click] So a bigger ship carries more for each unit of drag — enough coal for the whole trip.

So Brunel built the biggest ship ever

The Pesse canoe, the Batavia, and Brunel's first two ships — each the biggest in the world when launched. Then [click] he doubled it — twice the Great Britain. It hit its design speed — an amazing feat. It was also a commercial disaster. [verify dimensions and speeds]

You can't double a ship's size by trial and error

One mistake costs the whole ship. So Froude asked: how do we test the ship before it exists?

Froude's insight: don't just scale the ship — scale the physics

Waves are the physics that matter for a ship. An ocean swell 200 m long rolls along at 60 km/h, as fast as a car. A ripple a few cm long crawls. [click] Longer waves travel faster — and nothing else matters, just gravity and length.

And matching physical scale became a foundational idea in science.

Submarines don't make waves: so balance fluid mass and friction

Reynolds number = fluid mass × speed × sizefluid friction
Find the important ratio, and balance it. A submarine makes no waves, so gravity drops out. [click] What matters now is the fluid's mass compared to its friction. Air has a lot of friction for its mass. But squeeze it and it gets heavier while its friction stays the same. [click] At 15 bar it behaves like water. [click] Go further and it beats water: now a small, fast model sees the same flow as a full-size submarine.

Geological process too slow? Turn up gravity!

soil pressure = soil mass × gravity × depth
Soil and dikes: you can't wait years to see a dike settle. [click] Soil behaves according to the pressure it's under. Make the model 100× shallower, and you need 100× gravity to match. Spin it: 134 turns a minute on a 5 m arm. [click] And time scales too: ten years of a dike settling happen overnight.

Life follows scaling laws too

A mouse and an elephant. [click] Mouse to elephant, one straight line: the energy an animal burns grows like mass to the three-quarters (Kleiber, 1932). [click] In 1962, an elephant called Tusko, at the Lincoln Park Zoo in Oklahoma City, was given a dose of LSD scaled up by body weight — the dashed line. About 20× too much, and it killed him.

Now AI is being used to find the scaling from data automatically

Given only data, the machine found the ratios that matter for friction on a rough wall
Froude found his rule by insight. Can a machine find the next one? [PhD student's work.] Even a rough rule it found for friction on a wall works nicely for ships — and friction is a big part of a ship's drag. Keep it short (~1.5 min).

Going a little slower is much better for the environment

Marine diesel engines emit 20 million tonnes of nitrogen oxides every year: 50% of the pollutants in coastal regions
One more scaling rule, back on a ship. Drag grows with speed squared, and power is drag times speed: so engine power grows with speed CUBED. Steam made ships fast — and fast is hungry. [click] Go 20% slower: half the engine power, and about a third less fuel for the whole trip, even though it takes longer. Shipping really did this after 2008: "slow steaming" [verify]. [click] Half speed: an eighth of the power. That matters (on screen from the start): marine diesels emit about 20 million tonnes of nitrogen oxides a year [source: marine diesel review, White Rose eprint 164697]. [click] And once the engine needs so much less, the wind can do a real share of the work.
No one knows how to sail a 400 m ship!

Wind is back: free, clean — and never hungry

Call back to "hungry." Full circle to sails, but with 150 years of science behind them. [click] A rotor sail: a spinning cylinder that turns wind into push (simulation). [click] Suction wings from eConowind, a Dutch company. [click] Kites. The catch: wind is free, but fickle — and no one knows how to sail a 400 m ship!

Can we teach the ship to sail itself? Yes!

Wind is fickle: sailing well takes skill. Can a boat learn it? Nobody programmed it to sail — it learned by trial and error (reinforcement learning). Exactly what Froude said you couldn't do with a real ship. A simulator plus AI makes trial and error cheap again.

The AI sailor learned to sail by trial and error

Reinforcement learning: try something, see what happens, score it, adjust. Millions of times, in a simulator. Froude said you can't do trial and error with a real ship. With a simulator you can.

Unsteady things can still surprise us

Pay off STEADY. Kiki's boat, nearly stuck mid-tack (optional first beat — cut it if it drags). [click] Over-correcting a turn. And big waves. Every one is things changing faster than the ship can respond — exactly where towing tanks, built for steady, are weakest. [click] Simulations can now follow flow that changes moment to moment, like these breaking waves.

Nature perfected unsteady swimming 500 million years ago

Animals don't avoid unsteady flow. They use it. Look at the four flippers.

Swimmers and flyers all found the same ratio

Strouhal number = flap rate × flap sizespeed
Find the important ratio, one more time. For flapping it's how big the flap is compared to how far you travel per flap. [click] Dolphins, sharks, birds, bats, insects: all cruise in the same narrow band [verify: Taylor, Nudds & Thomas 2003]. Evolution found the ratio before we did.

Why are we still inventing ships?

After ten thousand years, there's still so much more to learn

The first boat, and one that taught itself to sail. Ask the title question again. [click] We keep inventing ships because we still don't fully understand the water they move through — and that's the exciting part.
